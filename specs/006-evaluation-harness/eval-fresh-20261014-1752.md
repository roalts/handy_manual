- statement: Before you recover or recharge refrigerant on an NK-600, check the serial number against open service bulletins and campaigns, because on NK-600 units in the EXV replacement campaign a sticking expansion valve produces the same alarm as a low charge and the bulletin takes priority.
- warning: Refrigerant can cause frostbite and, in enclosed spaces, asphyxiation, so you must wear safety glasses and cryogenic gloves when connecting hoses and ventilate plant rooms before work.
- warning: Never top up a circuit to clear a low suction pressure alarm; if you cannot find the cause, stop and escalate.
- step: 1. Read the alarm history on the controller first and note which circuit alarmed, when it happened, and the suction and discharge pressures at the time.
- step: 2. Check for non-leak causes of low suction pressure before you decide the charge is low: an iced evaporator, low chilled water flow, a dirty condenser coil, a blocked filter drier with a temperature drop across the drier above 3 C, or an expansion valve not modulating.
- step: 3. Inspect for oil stains at joints, flare nuts, service valves, the compressor, and the typical NK-600 leak points at flare connections at the transducers, Schrader cores, oil return line fittings, and condenser header joints, because oil marks the place where refrigerant escapes.
- step: 4. Run the unit and leak test both circuits with a calibrated electronic leak detector rated 3 g/year or better, moving the probe at no more than 5 cm per second and below joints because R-513A is heavier than air.
- step: 5. Confirm every detector indication with bubble solution before you open the circuit.
- step: 6. If no leak is found but the charge is low, do not top up; record the result, tag the unit, and escalate to the team lead for an ultrasonic or tracer-gas test because a charge that disappeared went somewhere.

- step: 1. Set Required certification = Confined space on the work order and get supervisor approval with both names assigned.
- step: 2. Ask Sierra Springs EHS for the site confined space permit at least 48 hours ahead.
- step: 3. Check the gas monitor is in calibration, and bump test it on the day of entry before you leave for site.
- step: 4. Check the rescue equipment: tripod, winch, full-body harness, retrieval line, and ventilation blower with ducting.
- step: 5. Electrically isolate and lock every pump that discharges into, draws from, or stands in the pit; for PP-1, close and lock the raw water supply valve V-101 and the suction isolation valve, and drain the suction line to the pit before entry.
- step: 6. Record every lock on the permit.
- step: 7. Test the atmosphere before anyone breaks the plane of the hatch by opening the hatch sample port, or lifting the hatch only far enough for the sample tube, and test from outside.
- step: 8. Test at three levels: just below the hatch, at mid-depth, and 0.3 m above the floor, and hold the tube at each level for at least two minutes so the pump draws a full sample.
- statement: For Fresno pits, the acceptable limits are O2 19.5% to 23.5%, H2S below 10 ppm, CO below 25 ppm, LEL below 10% LEL, and CO2 below 5,000 ppm; low oxygen at Fresno almost always means CO2 has displaced it, and CO2 and H2S sit at the bottom so a reading at the top tells you nothing about the floor.

- step: 10. When the readings are acceptable, the entry supervisor completes and signs the Corvane permit and posts it at the hatch next to the site permit.
- step: 11. Enter with a full-body harness, the retrieval line attached to the dorsal ring, and the personal gas monitor in the breathing zone, and keep the line attached to the winch for the whole entry.
- step: 12. Have the attendant log you in with the time.
- warning: You must speak with the attendant at least every five minutes, and the attendant must watch the blower, the monitor display where visible, and the yard.
- step: 13. Take tools and parts down in a bucket on a line, not carried on the ladder.
- warning: Leave the pit at once if the monitor alarms, the attendant says so, you feel dizzy, short of breath, or unwell, or anything changes in the pit; the attendant must order you out if the blower stops, a vehicle runs near the inlet, the attendant has to leave the hatch, or site alarms sound.
